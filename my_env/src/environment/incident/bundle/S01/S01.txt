PART 1 — PLANT CONFIGURATION ON 9 AUGUST 2019
P1  Hornsea One: offshore wind farm 120 km [reported] from the Yorkshire coast, under construction offshore;
    1.2 GW [reported] installed capacity when complete (174 [reported] turbines rated 7 MW [reported]).
P2  Three phases of 400 MW [reported] each: Hornsea 1A, Hornsea 1B and Hornsea 1C. Each phase connects to its
    own HVAC collector substation, then an offshore HVAC reactive compensation station, an onshore HVAC
    substation and the National Grid 400 kV [reported] substation at Killingholme.
P3  Two modules fully installed and the third partially installed: declared export capability
    800 MW [reported].
P4  Internal network outage configuration: one SGT and one offshore transmission system user asset (OTSUA)
    circuit on outage.
P5  Hornsea 1B (circuit Z12) operating at 400 MW [reported] before the event (maximum for that unit).

PART 3 — POST-EVENT ANNOTATIONS (added after the event; not contemporaneous log content)
Annotation A — operator investigation report, "Ørsted Technical Report for National Grid ESO on the
events of 9 August 2019", dated 5 September 2019:
A1  Section "Conclusion": "The de-load was caused by an unexpected wind farm control system response, due
    to an insufficiently damped electrical resonance in the sub-synchronous frequency range, which was
    triggered by the initial event."
A2  Initially the wind farm responded as expected by injecting reactive power; in the following few hundred
    milliseconds, as active power reduced and the reactive power balance changed, the majority of the turbines
    were disconnected by automatic protection systems.
A3  Hornsea 1B system voltage falls to ~20 kV [reported] while attempting to maintain full active power,
    which leads to an overcurrent of each WTG on 1B and 1C; the WTGs de-load as part of the industry
    standard protection system to avoid permanent damage to the generators.
A4  Hornsea One's systems identified a weak grid and then detected an unusual voltage disturbance, later found
    to have been caused by a circuit trip on the external 400 kV [reported] network, itself caused by a
    lightning strike. The operator states it is unaware of the exact time of the lightning strike.
A5  Identified after the event, as part of this investigation: electrical oscillations occurring before the
    event, without causing any de-loading. About 10 minutes [reported] before the event (log entry
    ~15:42Z [estimated]; exact time not published), the reactive power response to a 2% [reported] voltage
    step change was a damped oscillatory response from the overall voltage control systems.
A6  Before the event each stage had been modelled and physically tested in line with grid code requirements;
    the potential for oscillations had been considered. To the operator's knowledge this is the first time
    such oscillations have led to a de-load of a wind farm.
A7  A control system software update performed on 10 August 2019 [reported], previously agreed with the
    manufacturer, is stated to mitigate the observed behaviour.
Annotation B — operator statements as reported in the ESO technical report dated 6 September 2019:
B1  Initially the operator understood the Dynamic Reactive Compensator (DRC) to be responsible for the rapid
    de-load; it has since concluded that the DRC worked as designed and was not the cause.
B2  The configuration with one SGT and one OTSUA circuit on outage was a contributory factor as it created a
    weak internal network environment; the operator has since reviewed and reconfigured it.
B3  The wind turbine settings were standard settings from the manufacturer; the turbine controllers reacted
    incorrectly due to an insufficiently damped electrical resonance in the sub-synchronous frequency range,
    so that the local voltage dropped and the turbines shut themselves down.
Annotation C — regulator findings, Ofgem report dated 3 January 2020:
C1  The onshore control system operated as expected when the system voltage dipped; the offshore wind turbine
    controllers reacted incorrectly to voltage fluctuations on the offshore network following the fault,
    causing an instability that triggered two modules to automatically shut down.
C2  The stability issue had occurred around ten minutes [reported] before the incident without causing
    de-loading at that time.
C3  Modelling with the equipment manufacturer before 9 August suggested voltage control performance issues at
    the full planned 1,200 MW [reported]; these were not discussed with the ESO. The software update had been
    planned for 13 August [reported] and was implemented on 10 August [reported].
