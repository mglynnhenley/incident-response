SECTION A — SECURITY STANDARD (SQSS): SECURED EVENTS AND FREQUENCY LIMITS
A1   ESO 2.2: the SQSS specifies the limits of frequency deviations for secured events, which include
     loss of output from a single generating unit, CCGT module, boiler, nuclear reactor or DC bi-pole.
     Normal Infeed Loss Risk: maximum frequency deviation should not exceed 0.5 Hz [reported].
     Infrequent Infeed Loss Risk: frequency should not deviate outside the range 49.5 Hz [reported]
     to 50.5 Hz [reported] for more than 60 seconds [reported].
A2   ESO 2.2: the level of the normal infeed loss actually covered depends on the configuration of the
     system at the time (printed as "typically it is c. 1,000W" [reported]); the current normal
     infrequent infeed maximum loss risk is 1,260 MW [reported] for when Sizewell is at full load.
A3   ESO App. H: 'Normal Infeed Loss' is the maximum loss the system should be able to manage without
     the frequency going below 49.5 Hz [reported] (e.g. an interconnector at 1,000 MW [reported]);
     'Infrequent Infeed Loss' is a higher level (e.g. Sizewell at 1,260 MW [reported]) for which the
     frequency should not go below 49.2 Hz [reported] and should recover to 49.5 Hz [reported] within
     one minute.
A4   ESO App. H: the SQSS anticipates only one Secured Event at any one time and does not assume
     multiple Secured Events occurring simultaneously (N-1). The Power Infeed Loss is the power infeed
     that can be lost in one Secured Event: generally a single large generator, or two or more smaller
     generators connected through an asset treated as a single Secured Event (for example a double
     circuit overhead line or busbar).
A5   ESO App. H: the system should be re-secured as soon as reasonably practicable after the occurrence
     of a Secured Event.
A6   ESO glossary: "The current maximum upper limit for an infrequent infeed loss risk is 1800MW." [reported]
A7   Ofgem Box 4: the SQSS requires frequency to stay above or equal to 49.5 Hz [reported], or any fall
     below that level to be recovered within 60 seconds [reported], for such events; the ESO is required
     to secure the system by holding reserves so that the frequency limits are not breached.
A8   E3C final, footnote 14: the SQSS allows a deviation below 49.5 Hz [reported] provided that the
     frequency is returned to 49.5 Hz [reported] within 60 seconds [reported].
A9   Ofgem 3.2 and 3.5: the ESO does not consider it is required to secure against distributed generation
     losses under the SQSS; it follows an internal policy of securing against both transmission-connected
     and vector shift distributed generation losses for credible transmission faults during periods of
     increased risk (e.g. bad weather, lightning) or when it considers it economic to do so.

SECTION B — LARGEST LOSS, RATE OF CHANGE OF FREQUENCY AND VECTOR SHIFT (as ESO describes its practice)
B1   ESO App. H: in securing for the largest loss the ESO takes into account the protection setting
     historically specified in the Distribution Code for RoCoF of 0.125 Hz/s [reported], and seeks to
     ensure the system is configured in real time so that this limit is not breached for an infeed loss.
B2   ESO answer 11: the ESO secures the system to within a RoCoF limit of 0.125 Hz/s [reported] for the
     largest credible loss, and schedules frequency response so that the largest loss is contained to
     49.5 Hz [reported] for a normal loss and 49.2 Hz [reported] for an abnormal loss.
B3   ESO App. H: approximately 2 GW [reported] of small generators are connected to the distribution
     networks via relays which disconnect them if the RoCoF is greater than 0.125 Hz/s [reported].
B4   ESO App. H: approximately 8.5 GW [reported] of small generators are connected via relays which
     disconnect them if the vector shift angle is greater than 6 degrees [reported].
B5   ESO App. H: vector shift risks are divided into Type A (transmission faults with no coincident loss
     of BMU infeed, e.g. an overhead line) and Type B (transmission faults with coincident loss of BMU
     infeed, e.g. a generator busbar). The largest Type A risk is monitored for each half-hourly
     settlement period; Type B faults are only secured where it is economic to do so.

SECTION C — FREQUENCY RESPONSE AND RESERVE PRODUCTS (definitions as ESO describes them)
C1   ESO App. H: primary response must be delivering its required output by 10 seconds [reported]
     following the trip and continue for a further 20 seconds [reported] (30 seconds total).
C2   ESO App. H: secondary response must be delivering its required output by 30 seconds [reported]
     following the change in frequency and continue for a further 30 minutes [reported].
C3   ESO App. H: dynamic response is a continuously provided service to manage normal fluctuations in the
     frequency; static response is provided when the system frequency transgresses the low frequency
     relay setting on site. Both can come from generation and demand, BM and non-BM units.
C4   ESO App. H: Mandatory Frequency Response (MFR) can only be provided by synchronised BM units and is
     an obligation of the Grid Code; commercial response is procured from generation or demand, BM or
     non-BM units, whose providers are required to deliver the contracted amount.
C5   ESO answer 13: Firm Frequency Response (FFR) is tendered for on a monthly basis; Mandatory Frequency
     Response has similar characteristics but is an obligation in a generator's connection agreement;
     Enhanced Frequency Response (EFR) is a faster form of FFR; static frequency response reduces demand
     or increases generation when the frequency hits a trigger limit.
C6   ESO answer 13: reserve services: Short Term Operating Reserve typically delivers energy
     within 20 minutes [reported] or less; Rapid Start provides access to energy in less than
     2 minutes [reported]; Balancing Mechanism actions.
C7   ESO App. H: a minimum amount of dynamic response must be held for steady state frequency control;
     the minimum dynamic response requirement at the time of the incident was set to 550 MW [reported].

SECTION D — LOW FREQUENCY DEMAND DISCONNECTION (LFDD)
D1   ESO 4.3: the Grid Code requires each DNO to make arrangements that will enable automatic
     disconnection of demand if the frequency on the transmission system drops below
     48.8 Hz [reported]; demand is disconnected in staged blocks (5%, 7.5% and 10%) [reported].
D2   E3C final, Table 1 "Low Frequency Demand Disconnection settings for each frequency disconnection
     stages" (% of demand disconnection in each transmission area; an area is listed only where the
     table prints a value): E&W = England & Wales (NGET); SPT = Southern Scotland; SHET = Northern Scotland
     Stage 1 | 48.80 Hz [reported] | E&W 5 [reported]
     Stage 2 | 48.75 Hz [reported] | E&W 5 [reported]
     Stage 3 | 48.70 Hz [reported] | E&W 10 [reported]
     Stage 4 | 48.60 Hz [reported] | E&W 7.5 [reported] | SHET 10 [reported]
     Stage 5 | 48.50 Hz [reported] | E&W 7.5 [reported] | SPT 10 [reported]
     Stage 6 | 48.40 Hz [reported] | E&W 7.5 [reported] | SPT 10 [reported] | SHET 10 [reported]
     Stage 7 | 48.20 Hz [reported] | E&W 7.5 [reported] | SPT 10 [reported] | SHET 10 [reported]
     Stage 8 | 48.00 Hz [reported] | E&W 5 [reported] | SPT 10 [reported] | SHET 10 [reported]
     Stage 9 | 47.80 Hz [reported] | E&W 5 [reported]
     Total % demand | E&W 60 [reported] | SPT 40 [reported] | SHET 40 [reported]
D3   Ofgem 3.38: Grid Code Operating Code 6 requires each DNO in England and Wales to have LFDD equipment
     in place to disconnect a maximum of 60% [reported] of demand (measured at winter demand), operating
     in stages; the requirements contain an accuracy tolerance for the frequency levels and a time delay
     permitted for different LFDD equipment, depending on its installation date.
D4   Ofgem 3.39: Stage 1 of the LFDD required 5% [reported] of winter peak demand to be disconnected.
D5   E3C final, on the 5% [reported] figure: "The 5% demand figure is based on forecasted peak demand
     during an average cold spell."
D6   E3C interim: the scheme is designed to disconnect between 5 - 60% [reported] of total peak demand
     through 9 [reported] stages as the system frequency reaches certain trigger levels.
D7   ESO glossary: LFDD is set up in agreement with the ESO by DNOs to reduce their demand in
     5% [reported] stages of total demand (printed as "up to 40% available") [reported].
D8   Ofgem 3.44: SP Distribution, being part of the SP Transmission network area, is not required by the
     Grid Code to disconnect demand until frequency reaches 48.5 Hz [reported].
D9   ESO 2.5: Grid Code OC6.6 describes the automatic LFDD scheme and the arrangements the DNOs are
     required to make in relation to it; following operation of the scheme, the DNOs must notify ESO of
     its operation and are not permitted to restore automatically disconnected demand without
     instruction from National Grid ESO.
D10  Ofgem 3.40: the Grid Code does not currently regulate the disconnection of distributed generation
     through LFDD.

SECTION E — DISTRIBUTED GENERATION PROTECTION (Distribution Code, as the reports describe it)
E1   E3C final: under the Distribution Code, embedded generators are required to install protection
     which detects when the generator is islanded and safely shuts the plant down, predominantly by
     monitoring the rate of change of frequency or the change in voltage phase angle (vector shift).
E2   E3C final: requirements introduced in 2014 [reported] required all generators with a capacity
     greater than 5 MW [reported] to install RoCoF protection with specific settings; this was
     implemented across all relevant sites in 2018 [reported], and the Accelerated Loss of Mains
     Protection programme was initiated in 2019 [reported] for embedded generators between
     5 MW and 50 kW [reported].
E3   Ofgem, footnote 18: vector shift protection is no longer permitted for distributed generators
     connected after 1 February 2018 [reported].
E4   Ofgem 3.32: Distribution Code changes reduced the frequency level triggering under frequency
     protections to 47 Hz [reported], permitted for distributed generators with capacities greater
     than 5 MW [reported] from August 2010 [reported].

SECTION F — GENERATOR FAULT RIDE THROUGH AND VOLTAGE CONTROL (as the reports describe them)
F1   Ofgem 3.24: the Grid Code requirements on generators' responses to network faults (Fault Ride Through)
     require that generators remain connected and transiently stable following a fault on the transmission
     system, with power output recovering to at least 90% [reported] within 0.5 seconds [reported]; Ofgem's
     footnote cites Connection Condition CC.6.3.15.1.
F2   Ofgem 3.24: the Grid Code requires an overall voltage control system which appropriately dampens or
     limits swings; Ofgem's footnote cites Connection Condition CC.A.7.2.5.2, Voltage Control (Oscillations).
F3   Ofgem 2.12: transmission-connected generators have a requirement to remain connected and continue
     providing power to the system following faults on the transmission network.
F4   ESO 2.4: generators that use the transmission networks are signatories to the CUSC, which places an
     obligation on them to meet the requirements of the Grid Code, including any site-specific criteria in
     their connection agreement. The Grid Code sets out the capabilities a generator must have, including
     voltage control and frequency control, and states what a generator is expected to do during and after a
     network fault. While an offshore network and generation connection is being developed, the project is
     treated in the same way as a generator (Offshore Transmission System Development User Works, OTSDUW) and
     is subject to Grid Code requirements.
F5   E3C final: generators connecting to the transmission network are required to comply with the Connection
     and Use of System Code and the Grid Code, which set out the range of capabilities a generator must have,
     including voltage control and frequency control, and what a generator is expected to do during and after
     a fault on the network.

SECTION G — GENERATOR COMPLIANCE PROCESS (as the ESO describes it)
G1   ESO 2.4: the Grid Code sets out an extensive process for new generators to demonstrate that they can meet
     certain requirements to enable safe connection, detailed in its CP (Compliance Process) section; it is
     for the generators to assure themselves of their compliance with the Grid Code requirements.
G2   ESO App. G: generators are responsible for demonstrating and maintaining compliance with the Grid Code,
     both when connecting initially and on an ongoing basis, through a combination of studies, simulations and
     testing. The ESO runs the compliance process; for the initial connection of any large or directly
     connected generator, the generator proceeds through stage gates (energisation stage, interim operational
     stage and final operation stage), providing the required compliance evidence at each, and the ESO issues
     a notice to proceed through the stage gate if compliance has been met.
G3   ESO App. G: a generator that makes configuration changes which may affect compliance is responsible for
     first notifying the ESO of the change and then demonstrating compliance; the ESO is not responsible for
     checking compliance on an ongoing basis. If a generator believes it would require more than 84
     days [reported] to resolve compliance issues, the ESO issues a Limited Operational Notice (LON) and works
     with it to achieve full compliance.
G4   ESO App. G, footnote: the Grid Code defines a large generator as greater than 99.9 MW [reported] in
     England & Wales, greater than 9.9 MW [reported] in Scottish Hydro Electricity Transmission, greater than
     29.9 MW [reported] in the Scottish Power region and 9.9 MW [reported] offshore.
G5   ESO App. D glossary: EON, Energisation Operational Notification, is the first milestone in the
     commissioning process; ION (Part A), Interim Operational Notification for dynamic reactive compensation
     on OFTO assets, the second milestone; ION (Part B), Interim Operational Notification to export active
     power, the second or third milestone; FON, Final Operational Notification, the last milestone.
G6   ESO answer 45: there are no direct tests of generator ride-through for faults and large frequency
     changes, as these would require an intentional fault on the transmission system; the asset owner performs
     simulation studies, and the ESO will not issue an Interim Operational Notice until it has reviewed these
     studies and determined that they are in line with Grid Code requirements.

SECTION H — DEMAND RECONNECTION AFTER LFDD (in addition to D9)
H1   Ofgem 3.43: Ofgem describes reconnecting sites "without being told to do so by the ESO" as not appearing
     to have technically met the requirements of the Grid Code; its footnote cites Grid Code Operation Code
     6.6.4.

SECTION I — INCIDENT NOTIFICATION AND COMMUNICATION (as the reports describe it)
I1   ESO answer 34: current ESO procedure states that, following an event of loss of supply, a clear
     communication to Ofgem and BEIS is essential and is recommended to be initiated as soon as possible, in
     line with the 'Energy Incident Reporting' requirement set by Ofgem dated December 2017; resolution of the
     incident remains the primary objective in all cases.
I2   ESO answer 37: where GB Transmission System Warnings apply to system conditions or events with widespread
     effect, the ESO notifies Users by fax in accordance with the Grid Code; Control Telephony is used for
     instructing Demand Control. The ENCC Power System Manager and duty officer hold an SO communications
     document with contact details for BEIS and Ofgem duty officers, TO and DNO control room managers,
     generation and supply companies, and media and escalation contacts.

SECTION J — LFDD AND ESSENTIAL SERVICES (as the reports describe them)
J1   E3C final, chapter 3: the primary purpose of LFDD is to stabilise the system, so the Grid Code contains
     no requirement for DNOs to afford particular groups of customers, for example essential services, any
     form of protection; DNOs have some discretion in how they disconnect demand, largely dictated by the
     architecture of the system.
J2   Ofgem 3.41: there is no obligation on DNOs to refrain from disconnecting essential service providers in
     their LFDD requirements; LFDD equipment does not operate at an individual site level, and individual
     customers operating essential services should maintain and operate back-up power supplies.
J3   ESO 5.3: the ESO report refers to Protected Sites under the terms of the Electricity Supply Emergency
     Code (ESEC) and records that a site may request its DNO to categorise it as a Protected Site under ESEC.
J4   ORR 2.4: 48.8 Hz [reported] is one of the LFDD bands at which the DNO sheds load to stabilise the system
     and to protect essential services such as the rail traction supply; it is the duty holder's
     responsibility to ensure backup systems are in place where loss of power may lead to emergency situations
     (for example generators, uninterruptable power supplies or specific contracts with the DNO).

SECTION K — RAIL TRACTION SUPPLY FREQUENCY (as the reports describe it)
K1   ORR 1.0 and 2.1: Railway Group Standard GL/RT1210 (AC Energy Subsystem and Interfaces to Rolling Stock
     Subsystem) and BS EN 50163:2004 (Railway applications — Supply voltages of traction systems) specify a
     lower frequency limit of 47.0 Hz [reported] for a traction power supply.
K2   ORR 2.1: an advisory note to the standard defines a narrower range of 49.0 Hz [reported] to
     51.0 Hz [reported] within which the train is required to operate normally; outside this range it is
     permitted to reduce performance or to disconnect the vehicle drives.
K3   ORR 3.4: the trains were introduced using the interoperability process imposed by the Railways
     (Interoperability) Regulations 2011, which requires compliance with Technical Specifications for
     Interoperability (TSI); BS EN 50163:2004 is mandated by the Energy TSI. ORR quotes its Note 2 (BS EN
     50163:2004+A1:2007 Clause 4.2): vehicles will operate only within frequency tolerances of
     49 Hz [reported] to 51 Hz [reported] in the 25 kV [reported] / 50 Hz [reported] range, and outside this
     range vehicle performance may be reduced or the vehicle drives disconnected.
K4   GTR technical review (ESO App. F), cause 3: the NR Electrification System Compatibility document
     NR/GN/ELP/27010 ("Guidance for compatibility between electric trains and electrification systems"), a
     Manufacturing and Supply Agreement compliance requirement, identifies that the supply frequency can fall
     to 48.5 Hz [reported] in extreme conditions.
K5   Ofgem 2.7: traction supply standards in the rail sector specify a lower operating limit of
     47 Hz [reported], but also refer to a narrower range of 49 Hz [reported] to 51 Hz [reported] within which
     trains are required to operate normally; outside this range they are permitted to reduce their
     performance or disconnect.
